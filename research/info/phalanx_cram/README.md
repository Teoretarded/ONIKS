Because naval Phalanx normally fires non-tracer AP rounds, its live-fire footage often shows almost no visible tracer stream in daylight — just muzzle flash/haze and the target being shredded. C-RAM's HEIT-SD rounds, by contrast, are designed to be visibly tracer-lit so operators and bystanders can see the burst and its self-destruct flashes.

## What a burst looks like

1. **Muzzle**: at 50–75 rounds/second the gun sounds like a continuous buzz/roar (often described as a "chainsaw" or "buzz-saw" sound) rather than discrete shots; muzzle flash from the Gatling barrel cluster is a rapid, almost-continuous strobe rather than single flashes.
2. **Tracer stream (C-RAM, HEIT-SD)**: at night, tracers form a dense, almost solid-looking line/rope of light rather than individual dots, because the round-to-round interval at 4,500 rpm is only ~13 ms — faster than the eye can resolve as separate points at any distance. Colour is typically orange-red to yellow-white depending on film/sensor.
3. **Walking onto target**: the radar-directed mount makes small continuous traversal corrections, so the tracer stream visibly curves/walks across the sky toward a moving target (incoming rocket/mortar/drone) rather than holding a fixed straight line — this is the CIWS radar-closed-loop correction, visible as the stream's path bending toward impact.
4. **Self-destruct flashes**: HEIT-SD rounds that miss detonate on tracer burnout — in night footage this reads as a field of small secondary sparkle/pop flashes scattered along and past the impact point/trajectory, distinct from the intercept explosion itself, and is one of the most visually distinctive C-RAM signatures.
5. **Smoke/brass**: sustained bursts produce a visible haze/smoke cloud drifting from the mount position; naval mounts eject spent brass/sabot pieces which are not typically visible on camera at range, only close-in deck photos show casings.
6. **Burst length/cadence**: real engagements are typically short bursts (a fraction of a second to ~1–2 seconds each) rather than one continuous long stream, both to conserve the ~1,000-round magazine and because engagements resolve quickly at these fire rates — expect stuttered/staccato bursts rather than one sustained beam in realistic depictions.

No public figures were found for exact typical burst duration in seconds (only round-count figures above) or for a numeric self-destruct fuze time; these remain undocumented in open sources.
