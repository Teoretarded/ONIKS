# RGM/UGM-109 Tomahawk

## Public specs
- Length: 18 ft 3 in / 5.56 m without booster; 20 ft 6 in / 6.25 m with booster attached. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomahawk_(missile))
- Diameter: 20.4 in / 0.52 m; wingspan 8 ft 9 in / 2.67 m (wings fold flush for the canister, deploy after launch). [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomahawk_(missile))
- Mass: 2,900 lb / 1,300 kg without booster; 3,500 lb / 1,600 kg with booster. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomahawk_(missile))
- Cruise speed: subsonic, ~Mach 0.74 (~570 mph / 880 km/h). [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomahawk_(missile))
- Cruise altitude: 98-164 ft / 30-50 m above ground level — very low, terrain-hugging. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomahawk_(missile))
- Range: varies widely by block, Block IV up to ~864 nmi. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomahawk_(missile))
- Propulsion: solid-rocket booster (commonly cited as a Mk 106/Mk 135-family motor) for launch and initial climb, then a Williams International F107-WR-402 turbofan for cruise. No public exact booster burn-time figure found. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomahawk_(missile))

## What a launch looks like (Mk 41 VLS, hot launch)
- **Hatch**: same explosive hatch-blow a beat before exit as the other Mk 41 rounds. [Wikipedia (Mk 41 VLS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_41_vertical_launching_system)
- **Exit**: booster ignites in-cell and the missile leaves at full thrust immediately, wings still folded.
- **Vertical rise**: climbs straight up noticeably longer than SM-2/SM-6 in most public footage before leaning over — Tomahawk needs enough altitude to safely nose over toward a low cruise profile. USS Sterett footage (image in images/, filename references "tip-over phase") documents this transition explicitly.
- **Pitch-over ("tip-over")**: a hard, fast lean from vertical to a shallow dive/level path happens within the first several seconds of flight — steeper and more sudden than SM-2/SM-6's gentle lean, because Tomahawk has to get down to a ~30-50 m cruise altitude quickly rather than keep climbing.
- **Wing/fin/scoop deployment**: wings unfold and the turbofan's air intake scoop deploys during/just after the tip-over, roughly coincident with booster burnout, per NAVAIR's public description of the sequence. [NAVAIR](https://www.navair.navy.mil/node/9031)
- **Booster separation**: spent solid-rocket booster drops away once the turbofan lights and takes over; the visible flame changes from the booster's bright, thick plume to a smaller, harder-to-see turbofan exhaust (subsonic jet, not a rocket).
- **Plume**: thick white-grey smoke column at launch, characteristic of a solid rocket booster; once the turbofan takes over the missile is visually much harder to track — a thin heat-shimmer trail low over the water rather than a smoke column.
- **Disappears fast**: booster-phase visibility lasts only a few seconds in ship-deck video; once it's over and the missile is down at cruise altitude it quickly vanishes from frame, low and fast over the water/terrain.
- **Cruise profile shape**: rocket-boosted vertical climb -> hard tip-over -> shallow dive to very low altitude -> long flat terrain/sea-skimming cruise leg under turbofan power. This is visually the most dramatic "shape change" of any of the six weapons in this set.

## Manoeuvrability / drag notes
No public g-load figures — Tomahawk is a cruise missile, not built for high-g maneuvering; it flies a pre-planned low-level route using terrain-following/GPS guidance rather than dogfighting an intercept. Speed is roughly constant subsonic cruise (~Mach 0.74) for most of flight once the turbofan is established; no public drag/speed-decay curve beyond the initial booster-to-cruise transition.

## Where sources disagree
- The booster motor designation is inconsistently reported across sources (Mk 106 vs Mk 135 naming appears in different places); Wikipedia's Tomahawk article does not commit to one figure for burn time.
- Cruise altitude figures (30-50 m) are typical/publicized values — actual altitude varies by terrain-following profile and is classified in detail.
